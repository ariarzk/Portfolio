BT-01/Governance/Case study

Investment Framework & Governance

A standard way to evaluate, approve and review capital — where none existed.

Role

Corporate Development & Finance

Domain

Capital allocation · Corporate governance

Period

2024 — in force

Instrument

Board decree · 2024

01

Situation

The business was chronically underinvested. Capital decisions were taken case by case: no standard for how an opportunity was justified, no stated limit on who could commit the company, and no obligation to come back afterwards and say what the money had bought.

It also made comparison impossible. Two proposals could not be ranked against each other, and neither could be ranked against doing nothing.

02

Challenge

The framework had to work for two audiences: formal enough for a director and a commissioner to rely on when the capital structure was at stake, and simple enough for a plant manager to write a proposal without a corporate finance team.

The business had no history of structured appraisal, so the framework also had to show people what a case for capital should contain.

03

Approach

It was issued as a board decree rather than a guideline, under the company's existing good-governance article, so investment decisions had to meet the standards on transparency, accountability and fairness the company already claimed. I drafted it in the Corporate Development role; it was approved at director and commissioner level.

The policy sets five things: what the company may invest in, who decides at each level, what a proposal must contain, how it is assessed, and what is owed back after the money is spent. It opens with a glossary, because the terms it defines were being used loosely.

Two permitted classes: fixed assets — modernisation, expansion, rejuvenation — and project investment, from new sites and products to distribution infrastructure, process digitalisation and environmental capacity. Anything outside them means changing the policy rather than working around it.

04

Execution

The proposal document — a business requirement document — carries the need, the cost–benefit estimate, the risks and their mitigations, the implementation timeline and the score. It is drafted by a manager or senior manager, or by a head under supervision, so the case is written by the people who will run the asset.

The VP convenes an investment committee, which includes an independent advisor to assess the asset itself — for a machine, its performance, maintenance cost and fit with the line. Appraisal runs across five dimensions: strategic alignment to the long-range plan, cost efficiency, operational impact and capacity, demand fulfilment, product quality, each with operational and technology risk assessed alongside.

Decision rights are written down. The CEO approves every investment; the commissioner approves only what changes the capital structure — bank debt secured on company assets — and reviews its effect on leverage and solvency. Board-level cases travel as an investment memorandum: thesis, business plan, structure, risk analysis, funding structure and financials. Each stage has a review deadline, so proposals do not stall.

05

Result

A company-wide framework covering the full investment lifecycle, from the first written case to the accountability report after commissioning — in force since December 2024, with periodic review.

  • Evaluation and approval standardised: the same criteria, the same scoring instrument and the same gates for every proposal.
  • Decision rights stated in writing, including the single case that must reach the commissioner.
  • Management visibility into rationale, expected return, risk and — through the accountability report — what the investment actually delivered.
  • Its first major test followed under the same rules: BT-02, the blowing machine investment.

BT-02/Investment/Case study

Blowing Machine Investment

The framework’s first major test — capacity for growth, justified and financed.

Role

Corporate Development & Finance

Domain

Manufacturing capacity · Capital investment

Period

Nov 2023 — Sep 2024

Type

Capex · debt-financed

01

Situation

Manufacturing was running out of room. Two ageing two-cavity blowing machines carried the bottle supply across three shifts, and they could not keep up: just under a third of output was being toll-manufactured outside the plant, at a cost per unit the company did not control and a quality it could only check after delivery.

Capacity was the limit on growth, and it cost money every month it went unaddressed.

02

Challenge

This was the first major investment to run under the governance framework written the year before, so it tested two things at once: whether the plant could add capacity, and whether the new process could handle a decision that size.

It also had to be financed. The company needed a bank facility secured on its own assets — the one class of investment the policy sends all the way to the commissioner — so the case had to hold up for a lender as well as for the board.

03

Approach

The case was built the way the policy requires: a business requirement document setting out the need, the cost–benefit estimate, the risks and their mitigations and the implementation timeline, then an investment memorandum for the board — thesis, business plan, funding structure, risk analysis and financials.

Appraisal was deliberately conservative. Cash flows were discounted against the company’s required return, and the case was carried on cost savings the plant could name — labour, maintenance, materials and the toll-manufacturing bill that would disappear — rather than on expected demand growth. That is why the approved return was only 4.35% — the upside was deliberately left out.

The scoring sheet was completed independently by three assessors at board level — the commissioner, the chief executive, and audit and compliance — across the six criteria the policy defines. Financing was structured alongside the appraisal, so the funding request and the business case reached the commissioner as one document.

BT-02 · Fig. 01
04

Execution

One four-cavity machine replaced the pair it was bought to retire. Output that had been going out to toll manufacturers came back in-house, and the line covered demand on two shifts where the old configuration had needed three.

Cost moved in four directions, and the report records all four: labour and maintenance fell sharply, the toll bill and the outsourced material cost went to zero, and electricity rose — a faster machine draws more power. Netted at the demand level the report uses, the plant’s operating cost for the same output fell by roughly seven-eighths.

Demand then ran about 50% above the forecast the case was built on, which explains most of the gap between the approved return and the realised one.

05

Result

Delivered at a tenth of a per cent over the estimate, financed exactly as structured, and reported back to the board and commissioners in a formal accountability report eleven months after the first works began.

  • Manufacturing capacity up approximately 4×, on fewer shifts.
  • Fixed labour requirement down by roughly 20 crew — about IDR 70 million a month.
  • Supporting packaging material down about IDR 3,600 per box — roughly IDR 150 million a month.
  • Toll manufacturing eliminated: output that had been made outside came back into the plant.
  • IRR 38.35% against 4.35% approved; payback three years against six; benefit–cost 1.42 against 0.23.
  • The first major investment delivered under the new governance framework — and the first to be closed out under it.

BT-03/Operations/Case study

Quality Improvement Project

A quality problem taken from customer complaint to controlled process — without missing sales.

Role

VP Manufacture

Domain

Quality · Process engineering

Period

2024 — 2026

Partner

LAPI ITB

01

Situation

On taking responsibility for manufacturing, the complaint register was climbing steeply. The second half of 2024 ran at roughly seven times the rate of the first — a deterioration in months, not a standing condition.

Complaints are escaped defects: what reached a customer and came back. They are the last and most expensive place a quality problem shows up, and they were the only signal available: no in-process or laboratory data existed to explain why.

02

Challenge

The cause was not visible from inside the plant’s own records. It sat in the water treatment process — filtration, disinfection, piping — where the operating practice was long established, assumed correct and never measured.

Two constraints shaped what followed. The plant did not have the analytical capability to settle the question itself, and the work would need production stopped in a business that still had to fill its orders.

03

Approach

Rather than guess, I commissioned a process and facilities assessment, as business sponsor, from LAPI ITB, the engineering consultancy of Institut Teknologi Bandung, and set its scope, the investment priorities and the success criteria.

Five engineering disciplines worked the plant at once — environmental, chemical, microbiology, mechanical, and electrical control and instrumentation. Samples were taken going in and going out of every process unit rather than at the end of the line, raw water and product were tested against the full regulatory parameter set, and microbial populations were characterised in the laboratory.

The findings then went through the company’s own investment route — a business requirement document and an investment case under the framework in BT-01 — so the fix was funded as capital work with a scope, a cost and a stated benefit, rather than absorbed into maintenance.

Visual evidence

BT-03 · Fig. 01
04

Execution

The action list followed the findings rather than the symptoms. Cartridge washing stopped and replacement took its place; filtration was tightened, with the pumping to carry it. Disinfection capacity was restored to the maximum operating flow and given online measurement, so it could be checked rather than assumed. Process piping was replaced in stainless steel and the tanks cleaned; a further treatment stage was added downstream. The filling area was closed up — hygienic housekeeping under standard procedure, positive pressure, filtered air.

Sequencing was the hard part. Each of those steps needs the line down, and the plant was still filling orders, so the work was staged around production.

05

Result

The register turned in the last quarter of 2025, once the facility and process work was in, and 2026 has held. On the same January to July window either side, the complaint rate fell from 35.0 to 13.0 PPM and complaint events from 345 to 65. The most recent months run between roughly one and five PPM, against 2025 months that reached the fifties.

  • Complaint rate down 63% and complaint events down 81%, like-for-like.
  • Cost of poor quality down from 2.46% of revenue to 0.98%, like-for-like — a peak month of 3.47% in 2025 against a best month of 0.77% in 2026.
  • Failure cost moved inside the plant: external failure fell 91% as a share of revenue, from two-fifths of the quality bill to under a tenth.
  • Quality is now a standing management view rather than a monthly count — the register runs as a module of the Terminal, with defect families, shelf-age patterns and recurrence after corrective action.
  • Sales fulfilment maintained through every stoppage the rebuild required.
  • One spike remains, in February 2026, from a single large event; it is in the chart rather than smoothed out.

Type

MT-03 Production Capability Line capability, capacity loading, constraints. LiveOperations
MT-04 Quality Intelligence Complaint rates, defect families and shelf-age failure patterns. LiveOperations

MT-10 Plant Map Interactive facility layout — zoom and filter by area. LiveFacility

There is no roadmap here, deliberately. An eleventh module needs no change to the first ten, and listing modules that do not exist would say nothing about the system that does.

01

Situation

Analysis was not the missing piece. Production, quality, finance, procurement and people each produced it — daily, weekly, monthly and ad hoc, each in its own file, each complete on its own terms.

What did not exist was a shared management view. The questions that mattered crossed every one of those files: what a unit cost last month, why it moved, and what it would take to move it back is a finance question, a production question and a quality question at once. No file held that question, and no one owned it. By the time the pack was assembled, the month it described was over.

02

Challenge

Reporting is a record. A decision needs a comparison, a driver and a consequence — and management time went into building the pack rather than discussing the business.

The constraints were fixed: no engineering team, no platform budget, no server to run one on. Parts of the data foundation genuinely do not exist — no downtime or loss-event data, no in-process quality data, no bill of materials that reconciles with finance material cost. Anything built here had to state those gaps rather than fill them with plausible numbers.

03

Approach

Design around the decision loop rather than around dashboards. Data, performance, diagnosis, decision, action — each stage a question the system has to answer, and each question with something that has to exist before it can be answered. Most reporting stops at performance. The work is in the stages after it.

Two rules shaped the build. Every module is a single self-contained HTML file with its data embedded, so it can be written by anyone, reviewed by opening it, and published without touching the core. And every number names its source.

That made it something the team could extend. A published builder brief sets the contract, and one review gate — beta first, live after — checks that a module runs clean, carries no data it should not, and fits the Terminal before it is published.

04

Execution

Ten modules across six domains, each answering one management question rather than displaying one function’s data. A control tower reads across them rather than down them — factory state, unit economics, cost bridge, product economics, performance matrix, and where the pressure is. Cost per unit decomposes into material, labour, overhead and water, and the bridge reconciles to the rupiah.

The reporting engine closes the loop. It assembles the manufacturing performance publication as a Word document for any period — month, quarter, half, year to date, full year or a custom range — rasterising eleven charts and zipping the package in the browser. The same engine runs from the command line for batches, so the file that was verified is the file the Terminal produces.

Verification is a harness of 46 checks in three groups: browser wiring, reconciliation of every headline figure recomputed independently, and coverage of every period type. It caught water missing from the cost-of-goods composition, where the shares summed to 97% under a total claiming 100%. A separate leak gate scans everything bound for the public site for values from the real dataset before anything ships.

The plant signs in through Cloudflare Access — an email one-time passcode, so there is no password for the system to hold — and Access passes verified identity through to the Worker, which stamps shared records with who made them. Publishing is one command.

05

Result

The plant now has one management view. Meetings open the Terminal instead of a pack, so the evidence, the driver and the decision are in the same place — at the cadence the business moves rather than the cadence a pack can be built.

  • Ten modules live across six domains, in daily use behind authentication.
  • The executive publication is generated for any period, not written — and the generator will not write a strategic initiative; those sections print as marked blanks, because a commitment is management's to make.
  • What the system cannot say is stated in the report itself, in every issue: no OEE decomposition, no cost of poor quality, no supplier risk score, no target or owner a source system does not hold.
  • The team can add modules against a written brief and one review gate; the eleventh requires no change to the first ten.
  • No framework, no build step, no runtime dependency — a stack a non-engineering function can keep running.
